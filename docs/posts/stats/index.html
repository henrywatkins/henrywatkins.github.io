Probability and statistics are essential tools for data analysis, machine learning, and scientific research. This reference provides a concise overview of key concepts, formulas, and distributions used in statistics and probability. These are some notes I have accumulated over the years as a reference when necessary. It is not exhaustive, but it covers some of the important concepts and formulas.

Probability

Probability of event \(A\) is defined as: \(P(A)\) and we can also define its odds as: \[ \text{odds:} \quad \frac{P(A)}{1 - P(A)} \]

Conditional Probability for event \(A\) given \(B\) is defined as: \[ P(A | B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \]

The probability of the complement of event \(A\), that is A not happening is defined as: \[ P(\neg A) = 1 - P(A) \]

The probability of the union of two events \(A\) and \(B\) is defined as: \[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]

The probability of the intersection of two events \(A\) and \(B\) is defined as: \[ P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B) + P(A \cap \neg B) \]

General Law of Total Probability

The law of total probability states that the probability of an event \(A\) can be expressed as the sum of the probabilities of \(A\) given each of a set of mutually exclusive events \(B\): \[ P(A) = \sum_{i} P(A | B_i) P(B_i) \]

Bayes’ Theorem

Bayes’ theorem relates the conditional and marginal probabilities of random events. It is a fundamental theorem in probability theory and statistics, allowing us to update our beliefs based on new evidence.

\[ P(A | B) = \frac{P(A) P(B | A)}{P(B)} \]

This can be interpreted as:

  • P(A): a priori prob. → Probability of outcome A
  • P(B|A): Likelihood of B given A
  • P(A|B): a posteriori prob. → Probability of A given B (conditional probability)
  • P(B): Total probability of B

Distributions

For a random variable \(X\), we can define a distribution \(f(x) = P(X=x)\) which gives the value of the probability of value \(X\). Key properties of distributions

  • For a random variable \(X\), we can define a distribution:

\[ f(x) = P(X = x) \]

which gives the probability of the value \(X\)

Key properties of distributions:

Expectation value:

Discrete:

\[ E[X] = \sum x_i \cdot f(x_i) \]

Continuous:

\[ E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x) \, dx \]

Expectation value \[ E[g(X)] = \sum_i g_i(x_i) f(x_i) \]

or for a continuous distribution

\[ E[g(X)] = \int g(x) f(x) dx \]

The mean is just the expectation value of the variable itself

Variance

\[ \text{Var}[X] = E[(X - \mu)^2] = E[X^2] - \mu^2 \quad \text{where } \mu = E[X] \]

Correlation

\[ \text{Corr}(X, Y) = \frac{\text{Cov}[X, Y]}{\sqrt{\text{Var}[X] \text{Var}[Y]}} = \frac{E[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)]}{\sqrt{\text{Var}[X] \text{Var}[Y]}} \]

Covariance

  • If we have random variables that are not independent, there will be some correlation.
  • For two random variables \(X\) and \(Y\), the covariance is:

\[ \text{Cov}[X, Y] = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)] = \langle xy \rangle - \langle x \rangle \langle y \rangle \]

Cumulative Distribution

\[ F(x) = P(X \leq x) \]

\[ F(x) = \begin{cases} \sum_{x_i \leq x} f(x_i) & \text{(discrete)} \\ \int_a^x f(u)\,du & \text{(continuous)} \end{cases} \]

Mode

The value of X where the PDF \(f(x)\) has its greatest value:

\[ f(x_{\text{mode}}) = f_{\text{max}} \]

Median

The value of X where the CDF \(F(x) = \frac{1}{2}\):

\[ F(x_{\text{median}}) = \frac{1}{2} \]

Related are the upper and lower quartiles:

\[ F(Q_1) = \frac{1}{4}, \quad F(Q_3) = \frac{3}{4} \]

Spearman Correlation

The Pearson correlation between ranked variables

\[ \rho_s = \frac{\text{Cov}(g_x, g_y)}{\sigma_{g_x} \sigma_{g_y}} \]

  • Spearman finds monotonic relationships between variables.
  • Pearson finds linear only.

Moments

Higher powers of X:

\[ E[x^k] = \langle x^k \rangle = \int x^k f(x)\,dx \]

Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem states that the sum of a large number of independent random variables, regardless of their distribution, will tend to follow a normal distribution. The sum of independently sampled variables from any distribution is itself normally distributed with mean \(n\mu\) and variance \(n\sigma^2\). For example, after many observations, a sample is found and the mean is calculated. As this procedure is repeated, the average sample distribution will tend to a normal distribution.

  • \(\mu\): original distribution mean
  • \(\sigma^2\): original distribution variance

So we get a normally distributed variable:

\[ Z = \frac{X - n\mu}{\sigma \sqrt{n}} \quad \text{where} \quad X = \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \]

Sample mean as:

\[ \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \Rightarrow Z = \frac{n\bar{X} - n\mu}{\sigma \sqrt{n}} \]

\[ \Rightarrow \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \Rightarrow \text{Sample mean is normally distributed} \]

With: * Expectation = \(\mu\) * Standard deviation = \(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)

Important Distributions

Discrete Distributions

Binomial Distribution

  • Processes that consist of \(N\) trials with a binary outcome: success or failure (q).
  • If the test is performed \(N\) times, the number of successful tests follows the binomial distribution.
  • Probability of getting \(x\) successes from \(n\) trials:

\[ P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k} \]

\[ E[X] = np \quad \text{Var}[X] = np(1 - p) \]

  • \(P(\text{success}) = p\)
  • \(P(\text{failure}) = q\)

\[ \binom{n}{x} = \frac{n!}{x! (n - x)!} \]

The binomial coefficient is itself very useful in combinatorics, as it counts the number of ways to choose \(x\) successes from \(n\) trials.

Combinations of \(n\) objects into \(k\) groups:

\[ \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{(n - k)! \, k!} \]

  • \(n\) objects have \(n!\) possible permutations
  • We multiply combinations to get total set size

e.g. when removing sample in combinations: Total number of combinations:

\[ \binom{n}{k_1} \binom{n - k_1}{k_2} \binom{n - k_2}{k_3} \ldots \binom{}{k_l} \]

Hypergeometric Distribution

  • If the trials are not independent:

\[ P(X = x) = \frac{\binom{M}{x} \binom{N - M}{n - x}}{\binom{N}{n}} \]

  • Used when sampling is done without replacement
  • E.g. lottery or deck number probabilities
  • Probabilities change as trials are performed!

Poisson Distribution

  • For discrete events occurring in a continuum, with a mean rate of occurrence \(\lambda\).
  • In some interval or area, the Poisson distribution gives the probability of \(x\) events happening:

\[ f(x) = P(X = x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^x}{x!} \]

\[ P(X = k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!} \]

\[ E[X] = \lambda \quad \text{Var}[X] = \lambda \]

  • Probability of \(k\) events in interval with mean occurrence of \(\lambda\) over that interval.

Continuous Distributions

Gaussian or Normal Distribution

The Gaussian distribution is the most common distribution in statistics.

\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) \]

\[ E[X] = \mu \quad \text{Var}[X] = \sigma^2 \]

Cumulative:

\[ P(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{x} e^{-y^2/2} \, dy \]

Log-normal

If a variable \(X\) is Gaussian distributed, then \(Y = e^X\) is log-normal distributed.

\[ f(y) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi} y} \exp\left( -\frac{(\ln y - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) \]

Exponential

Describes the distribution of the length of intervals between successive events of a Poisson process:

\[ f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \]

\[ E[X] = \frac{1}{\lambda} \quad \text{Var}[X] = \frac{1}{\lambda^2} \]

  • Distribution of amount of time until event occurs.

Gamma Distribution

Describes the interval between every \(r\)th event in a Poisson process: \(\Gamma(r)\) is the gamma function, generalizing factorial: \(\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!\)

\[ f(x) = \frac{\lambda e^{-\lambda x} (\lambda x)^{\alpha - 1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \]

\[ E[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\lambda} \quad \text{Var}[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\lambda^2} \]

For collection of \(\alpha\) exponentially distributed processes.

Sample statistics

With \(N\) measurements (samples of a population) \(x_1, \dots, x_n\), a function of these data is a sample statistic. The most important sample statistics are the mean, variance, and covariance. These are used to estimate the population parameters.

Arithmetic mean:

\[ \bar{x} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i \]

Geometric mean:

\[ \bar{x}_g = \left( \prod_{i=1}^{N} x_i \right)^{\frac{1}{N}} \]

Harmonic mean:

\[ \bar{x}_h = \frac{N}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{x_i}} \]

Root Mean Square (RMS):

\[ \bar{x}_{\text{rms}} = \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i^2 \right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \]

Variance:

\[ s^2 = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = \overline{x^2} - \bar{x}^2 \]

Covariance:

\[ V_{xy} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) = \overline{xy} - \bar{x}\bar{y} \]

Correlation:

\[ r_{xy} = \frac{V_{xy}}{s_x s_y} \]

Estimators

The purpose of statistics is to infer the unknown population probability density function \(P(x_1, \dots, x_n)\) from the sample data. Parameters like \(a\) are characteristics of the entire population (e.g., mean, variance) and are derived from the full dataset. Estimators (denoted as \(\hat{a}\)) are sample statistics that aim to approximate the true population parameters \(a\). A good estimator \(\hat{a}\) is judged based on: Consistency, Bias and Efficiency.

  • Consistency:

    \[ \lim_{N \to \infty} \hat{a} \to a \]

    → \(\hat{a}\) tends to \(a\) in the large sample limit.

  • Bias:

    \[ B(a) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{a}] - a \]

    \[ \mathbb{E}[\hat{a}] = \int \hat{a} \, P(\hat{a}|a) \, d\hat{a} = \int \hat{a}(x) \, P(x|a) \, d^n x \]

  • Efficiency:

    \[ e = \frac{V_{\text{min}}}{V[\hat{a}]} \]

    → An estimator with low variance is more efficient.

    \[ V_{\text{min}} = \frac{\left(1 + \frac{\partial B}{\partial a}\right)^2}{\mathbb{E} \left[ -\frac{\partial^2 \log P}{\partial a^2} \right]} \]

    (includes bias and likelihood \(P(x|a)\))

  • Fisher’s Inequality:

    \[ V[\hat{a}] \geq V_{\text{min}} \]

    → Sets a lower limit on the variance of any estimator.

The most important estimators are:

  • Mean, variance, standard deviation, moments
  • Central moments, covariance, correlation

Errors

The estimators we get from sample statistics have errors associated with them because we have only partial information about the population. We characterise this uncertainty with the standard deviation of the sampling distribution:

\[ \hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{V[\hat{a}]} \]

Standard error \(\hat{\sigma}\) depends on the true values of the quantities of \(a\), so we must use the estimated error \(\hat{\sigma}\). So:

\[ a = \hat{a} \pm \hat{\sigma} \]

Example: The sample mean \(\bar{x}\) is found and has variance

\[ V[\bar{x}] = \frac{\sigma^2}{N} \] and so: \[ \hat{\mu} = \bar{x} \pm \frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{N}} \] (we draw values in this example from a Gaussian distribution with \(\sigma = 1\), and so \(\hat{\sigma}\) is known)

Hypothesis tests

Hypothesis testing is a statistical method used to make inferences about a population based on sample data. It involves formulating a null hypothesis and an alternative hypothesis, then using sample data to determine whether to accept or reject the null hypothesis.

  • Use the data to give a simple yes/no answer to a question.
  • Begin with a null hypothesis \(H_0\), and perform a statistical test to accept or reject it. Choose between \(H_0\) and an alternative hypothesis \(H_1\).
  • We define a test statistic \(t(x)\) based on data \(x\).
  • We determine the sampling distribution \(P(t|H_0)\).
  • We define a rejection region that contains some fraction \(\alpha\) of the total probability.
  • If \(t\) lies in the rejection region, then \(H_0\) is rejected, otherwise \(H_0\) is accepted.
  • When sampling from a Gaussian distribution, the Student’s t-test is used for hypotheses (The equivalent for standard deviation \(\sigma\) is the Fisher F-test): \[ H_0: \mu = \mu_0 \quad \text{vs.} \quad H_1: \mu \ne \mu_0 \]

Statistical significance

Is the probability of finding a deviation from the null hypothesis, also called the p-value. If \(p < 0.05\) (or other chosen value), we reject the null hypothesis.Null hypothesis = no relationship, no effect, “status quo”

Goodness of Fit (Chi-squared test)

A particular type of hypothesis test that determines how well a statistical model fits a set of observations. It is used to assess whether the observed data deviates significantly from the expected data under the null hypothesis. Given a model \(y = f(x_i; a)\) of some data that provides a relationship between variables, how good is the fit? (\(H_0\) or \(H_1\)) * Perform the χ² test * The test statistic is \[ \chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \frac{y_i - f(x_i; a)}{\sigma_i} \right)^2 \] Where: * \(z_i = y_i - f(x_i; a)\) * \(a\) = estimators of model * The sampling distribution of the quantity \(\chi^2\) will follow a chi-squared distribution with \(N - M\) degrees of freedom (M = constraints). \[ \mathbb{E}[\chi^2(\hat{a})] = N - M, \quad \text{Var}[\chi^2(\hat{a})] = 2(N - M) \]

  • We can perform a one/two-tailed test on the value of \(\chi^2(a)\) for our model.
  • For significance level \(\alpha\), the rejection region \(\chi^2(a) > k\)

\[ \int_k^\infty P(\chi^2_n) \, d\chi^2 = \alpha \]

Where \(P(\chi^2_n)\) is the PDF of the chi-squared distribution for \(n = N - M\) degrees of freedom.

Chi-square distribution

\[ P(\chi^2_n = x) = f(x) = \left[ \frac{(\chi^2_{n/2})}{\Gamma(n/2)} \right] x^{n/2 - 1} e^{-x/2} \]

Student’s t-test

The Student’s t-test is a statistical test used to determine if there is a significant difference between the means of two groups, especially when the sample sizes are small and the population standard deviation is unknown.

  • Samples \(x_i\) drawn from Gaussian distribution with \(\mu, \sigma^2\)

  • Want to distinguish the hypotheses: \[ H_0: \mu = \mu_0, \quad 0 < \sigma^2 < \infty \quad \text{vs.} \quad H_1: \mu \ne \mu_0 \] \(H_0\): null for some given \(\mu_0\)

  • The test statistic is: \[ t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{N - 1}} \] Where:

  • \(\bar{x}\) = sample mean

  • \(s^2\) = sample variance

  • We reject \(H_0\) if \(t\) falls into the rejection region defined by a critical value \(c\): \[ |t| > c \]

  • We must now find the sampling distribution \(P(t|H_0)\) i.e., what is \(t\) under the null hypothesis \(H_0\)?

  • The joint distribution for \(\bar{x}, s\) under the null hypothesis: \[ P(\bar{x}, s|H_0) = C \cdot s^{N-2} \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{N s^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{N(\bar{x} - \mu_0)^2}{\sigma^2}\right) \]

  • For \(t\) above, it is: \[ P(t|H_0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(N-1)\pi}} \cdot \left( \frac{\Gamma(N/2)}{\Gamma((N-1)/2)} \right) \cdot \left(1 + \frac{t^2}{N-1} \right)^{-N/2} \] → \(N-1\) degrees of freedom

  • As \(N \to \infty\), this tends to the normal distribution.

Cumulative distribution:

\[ C_{N-1}(t) = \int_0^t P(t'|H_0) dt' \] * For some significance level \(\alpha\), the rejection region is defined by: \[ C_{N-1}(t_{\text{crit}}) = 1 - \frac{\alpha}{2} \]

Fisher’s F test (ANOVA)

The Fisher F-test is used to compare the variances of two populations to determine if they are significantly different. It is commonly used in Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). * The previous test relied on the assumption that the parent distribution had variance \(\sigma^2\). * Now we can test this hypothesis using the F test. * Samples \(x_i\), \(N\) drawn from Gaussian with unknown \(\mu, \sigma^2\) * Want to distinguish: \(H_0 : \sigma^2 = \sigma_0^2 \quad \text{or} \quad H_1 : \sigma^2 \ne \sigma_0^2\) for a given \(\sigma_0\). * From the ratio of the likelihood distributions \(\lambda(u)\), we can define the variable: \[ u = \frac{N s^2}{\sigma_0^2} \] * The rejection region that corresponds to a two-tailed region: \[ 0 < u < a \quad \text{or} \quad b < u < \infty \] where \(\lambda_{\text{crit}} = \lambda(\bar{u}) = \lambda(b)\) * The sampling distribution of \(u\) is: \[ P(u | H_0) = \left[2^{\frac{N-1}{2}} \left( \frac{N-1}{2} \right)^{-\frac{N-1}{2}} \right] u^{\frac{N-3}{2}} \exp\left(-\frac{u}{2}\right) \] For significance level \(\alpha\): \[ \int_0^a P(u \mid H_0) \, du = \frac{\alpha}{2}, \quad \int_b^\infty P(u \mid H_0) \, du = \frac{\alpha}{2} \] The full F-test compares the variances of two distributions with \(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2 \quad \text{and} \quad \mu_2, \sigma_2^2\) * The test is to distinguish: \(H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 \quad \text{vs.} \quad H_1: \sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2\) * Samples: \(\bar{x}_1, s_1^2, N_1\) and \(\bar{x}_2, s_2^2, N_2\) * We define the test statistic \[ F = \frac{u^2}{v^2} \] based on the likelihood ratio of the two distributions. (Assume \(N_1 > N_2\)). The sampling distribution of \(F\) is: \[ P(F \mid H_0) = \left( \frac{N_1 - 1}{N_2 - 1} \right)^{\frac{N_1 - 1}{2}} F^{\frac{N_1 - 3}{2}} \left(1 + \frac{N_1 - 1}{N_2 - 1} F \right)^{-\frac{N_1 + N_2}{2}} \div B\left( \frac{N_1 - 1}{2}, \frac{N_2 - 1}{2} \right) \] Here, \(B(\cdot,\cdot)\) is the beta function. For a given significance \(\alpha\), the rejection region \(F > F_{\text{crit}}\) is defined by: \[ C_{N_1 - 1, N_2 - 1}(F_{\text{crit}}) = \int_1^{F_{\text{crit}}} P(F \mid H_0) \, dF = \alpha \]

Hypothesis test process

  • Define null (\(H_0\)) and alternative (\(H_1\)) hypotheses
  • Consider whether statistical assumptions are justified
  • Choose test and define test statistic \(T\)
  • Derive the sampling distribution of the test statistic under the null hypothesis given the assumptions
  • Choose a significance level \(\alpha\)
  • Compute the value of the test statistic from the dataset
  • Reject or accept null hypothesis based on whether the test statistic from the data falls in the critical region or not

Data type:

  • Categorical → Use Chi-square test
  • Continuous:
    • Normally distributed:
      • 2 groups → paired/unpaired → Student’s t-test
      • 2 groups → ANOVA
    • Skewed:
      • 2 groups → Wilcoxon-type tests
      • 2 groups → Non-parametric ANOVA

When trying to find relationships, as opposed to differences, regression models or correlation analysis is required.

Modeling

Modeling is the process of creating a mathematical representation of a system or phenomenon to understand, predict, or control its behavior. In statistics and machine learning, modeling involves using data to build models that can make predictions or infer relationships.

Generative + Discriminative Modelling

Discriminative models learn the decision boundary between classes, that is, it learns the conditional probability distribution directly \[ P(x|y) \] Generative models learn the distribution of each class. They learn the joint probability distribution \[ P(x, y) \quad \text{(also written } p(x \cap y) \text{)} \] if \(x\) and \(y\) are dependent: \[ P(x, y) = P(y | x) P(x) \quad \text{(Prior)} \] We can then model the data as being generated from a lower-dimensional or sparse latent variable \(x\) ( \(P(x)\) ) \[ P(y) = \int P(y | x) P(x) \, dx \] And we can then do classification: we find \(P(y | x)\), the likelihood that \(y\) is conditional on latent variable \(x\). We can then do inference with Bayes’ theorem to classify \(x\) or \(y\) \[ P(x | y) = \frac{P(y | x) P(x)}{P(y)} \] This approach allows us to take advantage of the structure of unlabelled data – this is semi-supervised

  • Examples of discriminative methods are:
    • Logistic classifiers
    • Support Vector Machines
  • Examples of generative models are:
    • Naive Bayes classification
    • Markov Random Fields
  • Generative models generally outperform discriminative models when there is little data, because they can take advantage of unlabelled data via semi-supervised learning.
  • Inference on generative models has been difficult because Markov-chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC) simulations of the evidence were not used and usually too slow in large models (or with little data).
  • Improved inference models have appeared in the last few years including:
    • Variational Autoencoders (VAE)
    • Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)
    • Autoregressive models (AR)

Bayesian vs Frequentist

e.g. classification of data \(x\) into category \(z\) with model parameters \(\beta\)

Frequentist Bayesian
Discriminative \(p(z \mid x; \beta)\) \(p(z, \beta \mid x) = p(z \mid \beta, x) \, p(\beta)\)
Generative \(p(z, x \mid \beta)\) \(p(z, x, \beta) = p(z, x \mid \beta) \, p(\beta)\)
  • Bayesians model the joint probability of data and parameters
  • Frequentists model the probability of observations given the parameters
