What is the Higg’s mechanism?

Spontaneous symmetry breaking and the origin of mass
physics
Author

Henry Watkins

November 28, 2025

The discovery of the Higg’s boson at CERN in 2012 is undoubtedly one of the most important physical discoveries in my lifetime. The role the boson (and its associated field) plays in by providing mass to other particles is such a fundamental phenomenon that any study of quantum field theory is not complete without it. In this post are a few short notes detailing the Higg’s mechanism, and how the coupling of the Higg’s field with other fields provides their mass. For a thorough treatment of this subject and quantum field theory in general, take a look at Mandl and Shaw (2013).

Setting the Stage: Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking

Spontaneous symmetry breaking occurs when the equations governing a system possess certain symmetries, but the ground state (vacuum) of the system does not respect those same symmetries. This is only relevant when the vacuum state is not unique. For spontaneous symmetry breaking to occur, the vacuum expectation value must be non-zero:

\[ \langle 0|\phi(x)|0 \rangle = c \neq 0 \]

For this to satisfy Lorentz symmetry, it must be a scalar quantity.

The Goldstone Model

A simple example of spontaneous symmetry breaking is provided by the Goldstone model. Consider a complex scalar field with the Lagrangian:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \partial^\mu \phi \partial_\mu \phi^* - \mu^2 |\phi|^2 - \lambda |\phi|^4,\quad \phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\phi_1 + i\phi_2) \]

This Lagrangian has invariance under \(U(1)\) global phase transformations \(\phi \rightarrow \phi e^{i\alpha}\), but can break spontaneously depending on the sign of \(\mu^2\). We can rewrite this in terms of the potential:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2} \partial^\mu \phi \partial_\mu \phi + \nabla \phi \cdot \nabla \phi + V(\phi),\quad V(\phi) = \mu^2 |\phi|^2 + \lambda |\phi|^4 \]

For the theory to be bounded from below, we need \(\lambda > 0\). The behavior depends on the sign of \(\mu^2\):

  • If \(\mu^2 > 0\): No symmetry breaking occurs, similar to the complex Klein-Gordon field
  • If \(\mu^2 < 0\): We get the famous “Mexican hat” potential, which has a minimum at

\[ \phi_0 = \left( \frac{-\mu^2}{2\lambda} \right)^{1/2} e^{i\theta} \]

In this case, the vacuum state is not unique since we can choose any value of \(\theta\). By convention, we often choose \(\theta = 0\), giving us \(\phi_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} v\) where \(v = \sqrt{-\mu^2/\lambda}\). To study fluctuations around this vacuum, we introduce real-valued fields to measure deviations:

\[ \phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} [v + \sigma(x) + i \eta(x)] \]

The New Lagrangian and Goldstone Bosons

Substituting this expansion into the original Lagrangian yields:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2} \partial^\mu \sigma \partial_\mu \sigma - \frac{1}{2} (2\lambda v^2) \sigma^2 + \frac{1}{2} \partial^\mu \eta \partial_\mu \eta - \lambda v \sigma (\sigma^2 + \eta^2) - \frac{\lambda}{4} (\sigma^2 + \eta^2)^2 \]

When quantized, this describes two coupled real Klein-Gordon fields: one (\(\sigma\)) with mass \(\sqrt{2\lambda v^2}\), and one (\(\eta\)) that is massless. The vacuum expectation value \(\phi_0 = \langle 0|\phi|0 \rangle = \frac{v}{\sqrt{2}}\) satisfies the condition for symmetry breaking.

The field \(\eta\) is called a Goldstone boson; it has zero mass as a direct consequence of the vacuum degeneracy. This is a general result: whenever a continuous symmetry is spontaneously broken, massless Goldstone bosons appear.

The Higgs Mechanism

The situation becomes more interesting when we couple this to gauge fields, leading to the famous Higgs mechanism. In QED coupled to the Higgs field, we define the covariant derivative:

\[ D_\mu \phi = \partial_\mu \phi + iq A_\mu \phi \]

The coupled Lagrangian becomes:

\[ \mathcal{L} = (D^\mu \phi)^* D_\mu \phi - \mu^2 |\phi|^2 - \lambda |\phi|^4 - \frac{1}{4} F_{\mu\nu} F^{\mu\nu} \]

This has \(U(1)\) gauge symmetry:

\[ \phi \rightarrow \phi' = \phi e^{iq\alpha(x)}, \quad \phi^* \rightarrow \phi'^* = \phi^* e^{-iq\alpha(x)} \] \[ A_\mu \rightarrow A'_\mu = A_\mu + \partial_\mu \alpha \]

However, when we expand around the vacuum, there are too many degrees of freedom. The solution is to use the unitary gauge where we set:

\[ \phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (v + \sigma) \]

This gives us the Lagrangian:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2} \partial_\mu \sigma \partial^\mu \sigma - \frac{1}{2}(2\lambda v^2)\sigma^2 - \frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} + \frac{1}{2}(qv)^2 A_\mu A^\mu + \text{interaction terms} \]

The remarkable result is that we now have: - A Klein-Gordon field \(\sigma\) (the Higgs boson) with mass \(\sqrt{2\lambda v^2}\) - A massive vector field \(A^\mu\) with mass \(qv\)

We’ve transformed a system with a complex scalar + massless vector into a real scalar + massive vector field. This is the Higgs mechanism that generates mass for gauge bosons without breaking gauge invariance.
