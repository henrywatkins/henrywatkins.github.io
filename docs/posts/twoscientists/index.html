The two scientists: hammers and nails

Approaches to research
metascience
Author

Henry Watkins

Published

January 13, 2025

I’ve recently come across an interesting proposition: scientists can be broadly classed hammers or nails. What I mean by this is that hammers are defined primarily by their tools, or the approaches they use to tackle a problem or question. The nails have a very specific problem (or set of problems) and try to bring a broad range approaches to solve them. A good example of someone who optimized the hammer approach is John Von Neumann, the mathematician who played a role in many of the most influential scientific projects of mid-century physics. He was a mathematician and brought is unique talent to bear in a range of problems, from the Manhattan project to the foundations of computer science. The prototypical nail is perhaps Charles Darwin, who spent much of his life investigating the diversity of life, developing the theory of evolution through decades spent breeding and cataloguing the plethora of life he encountered.

Of course this is not so clear cut and one can be a mixture of the two, rather this is a way of thinking about the way you approach scientific problems (or indeed any research project). Consider how you yourself start new projects. It is a valuable exercise to see how your own habits shape your research, because there are pitfalls to be aware of in both paradigms. The concern primarily is about not being bogged down; one can spend too long learning new things for the sake of it, thinking one is making progress because learning a new tool. Concurrently, one could also flit from one problem to another frequently, never settling on one long enough to actually make a dent in it. This is the value of thinking about research in this way, to be cognizant of the errors in process that just lead to a waste of time. There is a comparison we can make with Aristotle’s law of the golden mean - excess in either direction can be detrimental. Too much hammer and one is holding a solution and constantly looking for a problem. Too much nail and one never solves anything, because one is so wedded to a particular problem you can’t move on, even if it is the right thing to do. Just leave aside and move on to greener pastures.

A question might be when to apply each paradigm? Is it reasonable to to stick to one throughout your career? I would hazard to say that early in your career it might be worth learning a range of different methods, and this would imply the nail approach, particularly as you are finding your niche, the subfield in which you build your reputation and scientific portfolio. Most researchers begin their career in a PhD program where we are provided with a problem, or at least sat within a larger group in which we have limited freedom to choose our problems. In this sense it is advantageous to begin in the nail paradigm at the start. Once we leave our PhD and start as a post-doc we are generally then expected to be capable at certain key skills within a larger scientific program, but now we are experts in a particular technique. Often this is why we were hired to that new group as a post-doc in the first place. This is where the hammer-style of research comes in. Later on, when one has obtained a grant or more independence the freedom that comes from this enables you to pursue your own approach.

The question now however is the relative merits of both approaches. The severe expectations of a modern scientific career require a considerable output of grants, papers and talks to build a reputation and find a permanent position. There is a very real emphasis on quantity in modern science. Being a hammer or nail is thus refracted through the need for productivity to the detriment of originality or creativity. Perhaps I am pessimistic; experiences vary considerably across fields and countries. In this position the hammer paradigm might be advantageous. You can apply yourself to many different projects, potentially building a large and impressive portfolio of results. On the other hand, the complexity of modern science and the interdiciplinary nature of advanced research projects favours those that can build a novel approach to hard problems. Here the nail approach is at an advantage, because one can pull several techniques towards a well defined goal. A underemphasised virtue of a well defined problem is how it plays in grant writing. If you can clearly define your project goals then funders may be more ameanable to opening the purse strings.

A final question then. What do you think you are? Is your current approach to research questions suited to your skills and experience? Would aligning youself to one or other of these paradigms help you?
