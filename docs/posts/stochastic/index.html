Brownian motion, stochastic processes and the Fokker-Planck Equation

How do mathematical models deal with random processes? How can we apply the familiar tools of calculus to systems that have inherently stochastic terms? Over the last few months it has been an interest to dive deeper into the mathematics of stochastic processes. These notes, partly for my own interests, cover some of the key concepts in this field. In particular, these cover the Langevin equation, Brownian motion, and the Fokker-Planck equation, which are foundational concepts in the study of stochastic processes.

The Langevin Equation

How do we explain random motion?

We begin with the Langevin equation, which models a dynamical system under the influence of random perturbations. The archetypal system is that of Brownian motion, where a particle, suspended in a background medium, displays seemingly random motion from many unseen collisions.

Einstein’s explanation of Brownian motion is an often overlooked, but no less impressive, illustration of his way of thinking.

Start with the Langevin equation for a Brownian particle. This is just the equation of motion with a friction term \(\gamma\) and forces \(\textbf{F}\)

Brownian motion

\[ m \frac{d^2\textbf{x}}{dt^2} = - \gamma \frac{d\textbf{x}}{dt} + \textbf{F} \]

This equation has a steady state solution (or fixed point) with

\[ \frac{d\textbf{x}}{dt} = \gamma^{-1}\textbf{F} \]

Here we call \(\gamma^{-1}\) the mobility. We model the force term as a potential gradient (and thus a conservative force) with random fluctuations. The result is a stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\[ m \frac{d^2\textbf{x}}{dt^2} = - \gamma \frac{d\textbf{x}}{dt} -\nabla \phi + \textbf{f}(t) \]

A trivial solution to this equation is one where there is no potential (\(\phi=0\)) and the medium is so viscous that effectively we can take \(m=0\). This yields \[ \textbf{x}(t) =\textbf{x}(0) +\gamma^{-1}\int_0^t \textbf{f}(t') dt' \]

To make this expression analytically feasible, we look at the expectation values and variances of the terms in our equation instead of the point values. The random fluctations of the function f mean we cannot know the exact value, so we only have information about the statistical behaviour of this function. We first assume that the expectation value of f is zero, \(\langle\textbf{f}\rangle=0\). This means \(\langle\textbf{x}(t)\rangle = \langle\textbf{x}(0)\rangle\). The mean position of the particle over time is the initial position, despite the fluctuations and movements around the space.

We define the variance of the particle position in terms of the correlation function \(\langle\textbf{f}(t'_1)\cdot\textbf{f}(t'_2)\rangle\)

\[ \langle(\textbf{x}(t)-\textbf{x}(0))^2\rangle =\gamma^{-2} \int_0^{t_1}\int_0^{t_2} \langle\textbf{f}(t'_1)\cdot\textbf{f}(t'_2)\rangle dt'_1dt'_2 \]

We further assum that as well as having a mean fluctuation of zero, the random fluctuationas are uncorrelated: in a sense the force impacted by later collisions knows nothing about earlier collisions. This is a Markovian system, defined as one in which \(\langle f_i(t_1)f_j(t_2)\rangle = 0\) for \(t_2-t_1>> \tau_{coll}\) (\(\tau_{coll}\) is the collision time). If the collision time is taken to zero, we can define the correlation function for white noise, also known as Gaussian noise.

\[ \langle f_i(t_1)f_j(t_2)\rangle = 2D \gamma^2 \delta_{ij}\delta(t_2-t_1). \]

If this expression is used in the integral for the variance of x above, we find \(\langle(\textbf{x}(t)-\textbf{x}(0))^2\rangle =6Dt\). We saw earlier the solution for the case when \(m=0\). Let’s now look for a solution when our particles have inertia, when \(m \neq 0\):

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \left( \dot{x} e^{\frac{\gamma t}{m}} \right) = \frac{1}{m} f(t) e^{\frac{\gamma t}{m}} \]

\[ \Rightarrow \dot{x} = \dot{x}(0) e^{-\frac{\gamma t}{m}} + \frac{1}{m} \int_0^t dt' f(t') e^{\frac{\gamma (t' - t)}{m}} \]

Since \(\langle f \rangle = 0\):

\[ \Rightarrow \langle \dot{x}(t) \rangle = \dot{x}(0) e^{-\frac{\gamma t}{m}} \]

\[ \Rightarrow x(t) = x(0) + \int_0^t dt' \dot{x}(t') \]

\[ \langle x(t) \rangle = x(0) + \frac{m}{\gamma} \dot{x}(0)(1 - e^{-\frac{\gamma t}{m}}) \]

And the variance more generally:

\[ \langle \dot{x}_i(t_1) \dot{x}_j(t_2) \rangle = \langle \dot{x}_i(t_1) \rangle \langle \dot{x}_j(t_2) \rangle + \frac{1}{m^2} \int_0^{t_1} dt_1' \int_0^{t_2} dt_2' \left\langle f_i(t_1') f_j(t_2') \right\rangle e^{\frac{\gamma}{m}(t_1' + t_2' - t_1 - t_2)} \]

Now using white noise correlation:

\[ \langle \dot{x}_i(t_1) \dot{x}_j(t_2) \rangle = \langle \dot{x}_i(t_1) \rangle \langle \dot{x}_j(t_2) \rangle + \frac{D \gamma}{m} \delta_{ij} \left( e^{-\frac{\gamma}{m}(t_2 - t_1)} - e^{-\frac{\gamma}{m}(t_2 + t_1)} \right) \]

As \(t_1, t_2 \to \infty\):

\[ \Rightarrow \frac{D \gamma}{m} \delta_{ij} e^{-\frac{\gamma}{m}(t_2 - t_1)} \]

  • If \(\Delta t \ll \frac{m}{\gamma}\), a particle will have a similar velocity
  • If \(\Delta t \gg \frac{m}{\gamma}\), it is unlikely to have a similar velocity

The kinetic energy is:

\[ \langle x(t) \cdot x(t) \rangle = \frac{3 D \gamma}{m} \]

The variance is:

\[ \langle (x(t) - x(0))^2 \rangle = 6 D t \]

We can now define the Einstein relation:

\[ E_K = \frac{1}{2} m \langle \dot{x}^2 \rangle = \frac{3}{2} D \gamma \]

But since \(E_K = \frac{3}{2} k T\) by the equipartition theorem:

\[ \Rightarrow D = \frac{k T}{\gamma} \]

This is the Einstein relation, and provides the link between the diffusion coefficient \(D\) and the mobility \(\gamma\) of a particle in a viscous medium. This Brownian motion system is quite simplified however; White noise has no non-trivial correlations other than \(\langle f_i f_j \rangle\), but other kinds of noise require different correlation functions, potentially needing more than two \(f\)’s. This requires higher moments.

Higher Moments

If we now consider the correlation function for \(N\) random variables, we can write for \(N\)-point correlations \[ \langle f_1(t_1) \dots f_N(t_N) \rangle = \langle f_1 f_2 \rangle + \dots \langle f_{N-1} f_N \rangle + \text{permutations} \] if the number \(N\) is even, and for odd \(N\): \[ \langle f_1(t_1) \dots f_N(t_N) \rangle = 0 \]

For one random variable, the distribution with only 2-point correlations is the Gaussian (our white noise distribution):

\[ P[\textbf{f}(t)] = N \exp\left\{ - \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dt \, \frac{\textbf{f}(t)\cdot\textbf{f}(t)}{4D \gamma^2} \right\} \]

The Gaussian distribution is the only distribution with the property that all of its moments are finite, and it is completely determined by its first two moments. This is a functional integral over all \(\textbf{f}(t)\), and is normalised by:

\[ \int \mathcal{D}\textbf{f}(t) \, P[\textbf{f}(t)] = 1 \] or some function \(g(x)\): \[ \langle g(x) \rangle = N \int \mathcal{D}f \, g(x) \, e^{- \int dt \, \frac{f^2}{4D\delta^2}} \] Where \(x(t)\) is the solution to the Langevin equation with \(f\). We can stop here and note the similarities between the mathematical form and path integrals, particularly in quantum mechanics from a previous post. The functional integral is a generalisation of the path integral, and we can use it to compute expectation values of functionals of \(f(t)\). There is a deep connection between statistical mechanics and quantum mechanics.

Let us now introduce the generating function \(\mathcal{Z}[\textbf{J}(t)]\):

\[ \mathcal{Z}[\textbf{J}(t)] = \int \mathcal{D}\textbf{f} \, P[\textbf{f}] \, \exp\left\{ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dt \, \textbf{J}(t) \cdot \textbf{f}(t) \right\} \] Where \(\mathcal{Z}[0] = 1\) This generating function is a functional of the random variable \(\textbf{f}(t)\), and it generates the moments of the distribution. The function \(\textbf{J}(t)\) is a source term that couples to the random variable \(\textbf{f}(t)\). If we compute \(\mathcal{Z}\), the correlation functions are the coefficients of the Taylor expansion in \(\textbf{J}\). For the one-point correlation: \[ \left. \frac{\partial \mathcal{Z}}{\partial J_i(t_1)} \right|_{J = 0} = \int \mathcal{D}f \, f_i(t_1) P[f] = \langle f_i(t_1) \rangle \] For the n-th point correlation: \[ \left. \frac{\partial^n \mathcal{Z}}{ \prod^n \partial J_i(t_n)} \right|_{J = 0} = \langle f_i(t_1) \dots f_i(t_n) \rangle \] So for the Gaussian: \[ \mathcal{Z}[\textbf{J}(t)] = N \int \mathcal{D}f \, \exp\left\{ - \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dt \, \frac{\textbf{f}\cdot\textbf{f}}{4 D \gamma^2} - \textbf{J}\cdot\textbf{f} \right\} \] Which, if we complete the square:

\[ \mathcal{Z}[\textbf{J}] = N \int \mathcal{D}f \, \exp\left\{ -\frac{1}{4 D \gamma^2} \int dt \left[ \textbf{f} - 2 D \gamma^2 \textbf{J}\right]^2 - 4 D^2 \gamma^4 J^2 \right\} \]

If \(\textbf{f} \rightarrow \textbf{f} = 2D \gamma^2 \textbf{J}\), then:

\[ \mathcal{Z}[\textbf{J}] = \exp\left\{ D \delta^2 \int dt |\textbf{J}|^2 \right\} \] And:

\[ \frac{\delta \mathcal{Z}}{\delta J_i} = 2D \delta^2 J_i \, \mathcal{Z}[\textbf{J}] \quad \text{(for } n = 1 \text{)} \]

\[ \frac{\delta^2 \mathcal{Z}}{\delta J_i \delta J_j} = 2D \delta^2 \delta_{ij} \delta(t_1 - t_2) \mathcal{Z}[\textbf{J}] + 4D^2 J_i(t_1) J_j(t_2) \mathcal{Z}[\textbf{J}]\quad \text{(for } n = 2 \text{)} \]

This approach can be extended to fields, e.g. the fluctuating boundary between gas and liquid:

\[ \partial_t h = \nabla^2 h + f \quad \text{with } h(r, t) \]

The Fokker–Planck Equation

The aim of this section is to move from single-particle motion to a probability distribution of particles. The Fokker–Planck equation is a partial differential equation that describes the time evolution of the probability distribution of the velocity of particles under the influence of random forces. If the Langevin equation is: \[ \dot{\textbf{x}} = \gamma^{-1} \textbf{f} \] for one particle in a viscous fluid, the distribution is: \[ P(x, t) = (4\pi D t)^{-1/2} \exp\left( -\frac{x^2}{4 D t} \right) \]

Which obeys the equation:

\[ \partial_t P = D \nabla^2 P \]

This is a simple diffusion equation, and it show that for the simple of a massless particle in a viscous medium, the probability distribution of a particle’s position diffuses out over time. For more general equations however, we need the Fokker–Planck equation. Consider the Langevin equation with a force term (represented by the gradient of a potential \(\phi\)):

\[ \gamma \dot{\textbf{x}} = -\nabla \phi + f \]

Then:

\[ \delta \textbf{x} = \dot{\textbf{x}} \delta t = -\gamma^{-1} \nabla \phi \delta t + \gamma^{-1} \int_t^{t + \delta t} dt'\textbf{f}(t') \]

Since \(\langle \textbf{f} \rangle = 0\):

\[ \langle \delta \textbf{x} \rangle = -\gamma^{-1} \nabla \phi \, \delta t \] and \[ \gamma^2\langle \delta x_i \delta x_j \rangle = \langle \partial \phi_i \partial \phi_j \rangle \delta t^2 - \delta t \int_t^{t + \delta \epsilon} dt' \langle \partial \phi_i f_j + \partial \phi_j f_i \rangle + \int_t^{t + \delta \epsilon} dt'\int_t^{t + \delta \epsilon} dt'' \langle f_i(t') f_j(t'') \rangle \]

Using the white noise expression

\[ \langle \delta x_i \delta x_j \rangle = 2 \delta_{ij} D \delta t + \mathcal{O}(\delta t^2) \] To derive this from probability, we start with a Dirac delta function for the probability distribution of a particle at position \(\textbf{x}'\) at time \(t\): \[ P(\textbf{x}, t + \delta t; \textbf{x}', t) = \langle \delta(\textbf{x} - \textbf{x}' - \delta \textbf{x}) \rangle \] Taylor expand the Dirac delta: \[ P(\textbf{x}, t + \delta t; \textbf{x}_0', t_0) = \left\{ 1 + \langle \delta x_i \rangle \partial_i + \frac{1}{2} \langle \delta x_i \delta x_j \rangle \partial_{ij}^2 + \dots \right\} \delta(\textbf{x} - \textbf{x}') \] And we use the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation: \[ P(\textbf{x}, t; \textbf{x}_0, t_0) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} d^3\textbf{x}' \, P(\textbf{x}, t; \textbf{x}', t') P(\textbf{x}', t'; \textbf{x}_0, t_0) \]

Take \(t \to t + \delta t\) and integrate by parts to remove the integrals:

\[ P(\textbf{x}, t + \delta t; \textbf{x}_0, t_0) = P(\textbf{x}, t; \textbf{x}_0, t_0) - \partial_i \langle \delta x_i \rangle P(\textbf{x}, t; \textbf{x}_0, t_0) + \frac{1}{2} \langle \delta x_i \delta x_j \rangle \partial_{ij}^2 P(\textbf{x}, t; \textbf{x}_0, t_0) + \dots \]

Using expressions for \(\langle \delta x \rangle\), \(\langle \delta x^2 \rangle\): \[ \Rightarrow P(\textbf{x}, t; \textbf{x}_0, t_0) = P(\textbf{x}, t; \textbf{x}_0, t_0) + \gamma^{-1} \delta t \, \partial_i (P \nabla_i \phi) + D \delta t \partial^2 P \] Also Taylor expand in time: \[ \Rightarrow \frac{\partial P}{\partial t} = \gamma^{-1} \nabla \cdot (P \nabla \phi) + D \nabla^2 P \] This is the Fokker–Planck equation for a particle in a potential \(\phi\) with diffusion coefficient \(D\) and mobility \(\gamma\).

Probability Flux and Conservation

It is possible to write this as a continuity equation:

\[ \frac{\partial P}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0 \quad \text{with } \mathbf{J} = \gamma^{-1} P \nabla \phi + D \nabla P \]

The steady state solutions with potentials obey \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0\) (i.e., equilibrium):

\[ P(\textbf{x}) \sim \exp\left( -\frac{\phi}{D} \right) \] If we use the now familiar Einstein relation, we can write this as \(P(\textbf{x}) \sim e^{- \frac{\phi}{kT}}\).

Consider a potential well, a one-dimensional parabolic potential with a minimum at \(x_{\min}\): \[ \phi(x) = \frac{1}{2} \omega^2 (x - x_{\min})^2 \]

If we begin in equilibrium:

\[ P(x, 0) = \left( \frac{\omega^2}{2\pi kT} \right)^{1/2} \exp\left( - \frac{\omega^2 (x - x_{\min})^2}{2kT} \right) \]

What we can see from this expression is that the probability distribution is Gaussian, centred on the minimum of the potential well. Particles will leak out of the potential barrier, because even if the potential well is deep, there is still a finite probability of finding particles outside the well. Take this leak rate to be small so the initial state is a bath. Close to this steady state we can define the flux stright from the expression for the probability

\[ J = \frac{kT}{\gamma} e^{-\frac{\phi}{kT}} \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( e^{\frac{\phi}{kT}} P \right) \] If we integrate this flux between \(x_{\min}\) and far away \(x'\):

\[ \int_{x_{\min}}^{x'} J \frac{\phi}{kT} dx = \frac{kT}{\gamma} \left[ e^{\frac{\phi(x_{\min})}{kT}} P(x_{\min}) - e^{\frac{\phi(x')}{kT}} P(x') \right] \]

The Kramers–Chandrasekhar equation

We can look at the diffusion in velocity space instead of position space. The Langevin equation in terms of velocity is:

\[ m \dot{\textbf{v}} = -\gamma \textbf{v} + \textbf{f}(t) \]

\[ \Rightarrow \partial_t P = \frac{1}{m} \nabla_v \cdot \left( \gamma \textbf{v} P + \frac{D \gamma^2}{m} \nabla_v P \right) \]

In equilibrium \(\partial_t P = 0\) so \(\nabla_v P = -\frac{m}{D\gamma} P \textbf{v}\). If we solve this in one dimension, we get the familiar Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution:

\[ P(v) = \left( \frac{m}{2\pi kT} \right)^{3/2} e^{- \frac{mv^2}{2kT}} \quad \text{since } D\gamma = kT \]

To find the full phase space evolution equation-where we calculate \(P(x, v, t)\), we start with the full Langevin equation:

\[ m \ddot{\textbf{x}} = -\gamma \dot{\textbf{x}} - \nabla \phi + \textbf{f}(t) \]

We can write this in a Hamiltonian form, where we split this equation into two first-order equations:

\[ \dot{\textbf{x}} = \textbf{v}, \quad m \dot{\textbf{v}} = -\gamma \textbf{v} - \nabla \phi + \textbf{f}(t) \]

From this version we get the Boltzmann–Fokker–Planck equation:

\[ \left( \partial_t + \textbf{v} \cdot \nabla \right) P = \frac{1}{m} \nabla_v \cdot \left( \gamma \textbf{v} P + P \nabla \phi \right) + \frac{D \gamma^2}{m^2} \nabla_v^2 P \] This equation describes the evolution of the probability distribution of particles in phase space, taking into account both the deterministic motion and the stochastic forces acting on the particles.
